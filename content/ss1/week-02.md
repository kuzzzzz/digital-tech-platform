# WEEK 2 — HISTORY AND EVOLUTION OF DIGITAL TECHNOLOGIES

**Sub-topics:** Early counting and calculating devices · Mechanical and electro-mechanical era · The five generations of computers · Evolution of communication and the internet · Digital technology milestones in Nigeria

## Behavioural Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. trace the development of computing devices from the abacus to modern artificial intelligence;
2. name at least six early computing pioneers and state each one's contribution;
3. describe the five generations of computers using technology, features and examples;
4. explain the evolution of communication technology and the internet;
5. outline key milestones in the growth of digital technology in Nigeria.

## Entry Behaviour
Students know what a computer is from Week 1 and from JSS. Most have seen or used an abacus or counting frame in primary school.

## Instructional Materials
Chalkboard, an abacus or improvised counting frame (bottle tops on wire), a vacuum tube or picture of one, a transistor from an old radio, an integrated circuit chip from a scrap motherboard, a modern flash drive or memory card, wall chart of the generations of computers.

## PERIOD 1 (45 minutes)

### 2.1 The Manual/Mechanical Era

| Year | Device | Inventor | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| c. 3000 BC | **Abacus** | Chinese/Babylonians | First counting device; beads on rods for addition and subtraction |
| 1617 | **Napier's Bones** | John Napier (Scotland) | Rods for multiplication and division; also invented logarithms |
| 1622 | **Slide Rule** | William Oughtred | Analogue calculation using logarithmic scales |
| 1642 | **Pascaline** | Blaise Pascal (France) | First mechanical adding machine using gears |
| 1671 | **Stepped Reckoner** | Gottfried Leibniz (Germany) | Could add, subtract, multiply and divide; also developed binary arithmetic |
| 1801 | **Jacquard Loom** | Joseph-Marie Jacquard | Used **punched cards** to control weaving patterns — the first programmable machine |
| 1822/1834 | **Difference Engine** and **Analytical Engine** | **Charles Babbage** (England) | Designed a machine with input, store, mill (processor) and output — the design of the modern computer. Known as the **"Father of the Computer"** |
| 1843 | Programs for the Analytical Engine | **Ada Lovelace** | Wrote the first algorithm intended for a machine — the **first computer programmer** |
| 1854 | Boolean algebra | **George Boole** | Logic of TRUE/FALSE (1/0) on which all digital circuits are built |
| 1890 | **Tabulating Machine** | **Herman Hollerith** (USA) | Punched-card machine that processed the 1890 US census in months instead of years; his company later became **IBM** |
| 1936–37 | Turing Machine concept | **Alan Turing** | Theoretical foundation of computer science and artificial intelligence |
| 1944 | **Harvard Mark I** | Howard Aiken | First large-scale automatic electro-mechanical computer |

**Memory aid for students:** *A Number Sold Pascal Leibniz's Jacquard Basket* — Abacus, Napier, Slide rule, Pascaline, Leibniz, Jacquard, Babbage.

### 2.2 Why the Punched Card Matters
The punched card is the bridge between the mechanical and the digital age. A hole meant "1" and no hole meant "0". Jacquard used it for cloth patterns, Babbage planned to use it for calculation, and Hollerith used it for national data. The idea that **instructions can be stored separately from the machine** is the birth of programming.

## PERIOD 2 (45 minutes)

### 2.3 The Five Generations of Computers

| Generation | Period | Main technology | Key features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **First** | 1940–1956 | **Vacuum tubes** | Very large (filled rooms), consumed huge power, generated enormous heat, used machine language, punched-card input, magnetic drum memory, very expensive, unreliable | ENIAC, EDVAC, EDSAC, UNIVAC I, IBM 650 |
| **Second** | 1956–1963 | **Transistors** | Smaller, faster, cheaper, more reliable, less heat; assembly language and early high-level languages (COBOL, FORTRAN); magnetic core memory; magnetic tape and disk storage | IBM 1401, IBM 7094, CDC 1604, UNIVAC 1108 |
| **Third** | 1964–1971 | **Integrated Circuits (ICs)** | Many transistors on one silicon chip; keyboard and monitor introduced; operating systems appeared; multiprogramming and time-sharing; further reduction in size and cost | IBM System/360, PDP-8, ICL 1900 |
| **Fourth** | 1971–present | **Microprocessors (LSI/VLSI)** | Whole CPU on one chip; personal computers; GUI and mouse; networks and the internet; portable devices | Intel 4004, Apple II, IBM PC, modern laptops and smartphones |
| **Fifth** | Present and future | **ULSI, Artificial Intelligence, quantum computing** | Parallel processing, natural language processing, machine learning, expert systems, robotics, voice and image recognition, quantum computers | IBM Watson, self-driving cars, AI assistants, voice-controlled devices |

**The trend across all five generations:** size **decreases**, speed **increases**, cost **decreases**, storage capacity **increases**, reliability **increases**, power consumption **decreases**.

### 2.4 Evolution of Communication and the Internet
1. **Telegraph (1837)** → **Telephone (1876, Alexander Graham Bell)** → analogue mobile phones → **GSM digital mobile** → smartphones.
2. **ARPANET (1969)** — the American research network that became the internet.
3. **TCP/IP (1983)** — the common language that allowed different networks to join.
4. **World Wide Web (1989–1991, Tim Berners-Lee)** — hypertext pages, browsers, URLs. *Note: the Web is a service that runs on the internet; they are not the same thing.*
5. **Web 1.0** (read-only static pages) → **Web 2.0** (interactive, social media, user-generated content) → **Web 3.0** (semantic web, blockchain, decentralisation).
6. **Cloud computing (2000s)** and **mobile-first internet (2010s)**.
7. **Artificial intelligence era (2020s)** — generative AI systems that write, draw, code and converse.

### 2.5 Digital Technology Milestones in Nigeria
- **1963** — Nigeria's first computer, an **IBM 1401**, is commonly recorded as having been installed for use in connection with the national census and government statistics work.
- **1970s–80s** — mainframes adopted by banks, universities, NNPC and government agencies.
- **1992** — the **Nigerian Communications Commission (NCC)** is established to regulate telecommunications.
- **2001** — **GSM services launch** in Nigeria, transforming access to communication; **NITDA** is established the same year to drive national IT development.
- **2007** — the **National Identity Management Commission (NIMC)** Act, leading to the NIN.
- **2010s** — spread of broadband and submarine fibre-optic cables; growth of Nigerian technology start-ups; JAMB moves the UTME fully to computer-based testing.
- **2020s** — the fintech boom (Flutterwave, Paystack, Opay, Moniepoint), the **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023**, cashless-policy expansion and the "Yabacon Valley" start-up cluster in Lagos.

## TEACHER'S HELPER NOTES — WEEK 2
- **Do not turn this into a memory-cram lesson.** Examiners rarely ask for exact dates beyond the generations. Emphasise the *pattern*: each generation made computers smaller, faster, cheaper and more reliable. A student who understands the pattern can reason out an answer they have not memorised.
- **Bring physical objects if you can.** A scrap motherboard from a repair shop costs almost nothing and lets students hold an integrated circuit. Contrast it with a vacuum tube from an old television or radio — the visual difference between "one tube" and "millions of transistors on this fingernail" teaches the whole lesson in ten seconds.
- **Common error:** students write that the third generation used "microprocessors". Drill the sequence aloud: **tubes → transistors → integrated circuits → microprocessors → artificial intelligence.** A chant works.
- **Second common error:** confusing the internet with the World Wide Web. Use the analogy — the internet is the road network; the Web is one type of vehicle that travels on it. Email and WhatsApp are other vehicles.
- **Be honest about uncertain dates.** Where sources disagree (for example the exact circumstances of Nigeria's first computer installation), tell students that historical records vary and that they should give the commonly cited answer while understanding the period, not defend a single date.
- **Time management:** the pioneers table alone can consume a full period. Put the table on the board *before* the class arrives, or dictate it as a note in Period 1 and use Period 2 entirely for the generations.
- **Enrichment:** ask why a 1950s computer that filled a room is less powerful than a ₦30,000 phone today. This introduces Moore's Law informally.
